Project Jaeger

Fall 2025 — present Python · Gymnasium · PyBullet · Stable-Baselines3 Ongoing

Jaeger is an attempt to get a six-degree-of-freedom biped to walk in a straight line, and to do it in a way that could eventually survive contact with real hardware. It's the project I've spent the most time on and the one where the most things have gone wrong.

The environment

I built the simulation myself rather than using an off-the-shelf locomotion benchmark, which meant writing the URDF model, setting joint limits, and tuning contact dynamics until foot-ground interaction stopped producing physically impossible behavior.

The most important constraint is one that's easy to skip: control signals are clamped to ranges a real actuator could actually produce. It's straightforward to train a policy that walks beautifully by commanding torques no servo can deliver, and that policy is worth nothing off the simulator. Constraining the action space early costs training performance and buys a chance at sim-to-real transfer later.

Control architecture

The policy doesn't drive the joints directly. Underneath it sits a physics-based PD controller that already knows roughly how to hold a pose, and the PPO policy outputs residual actions layered on top of it — corrections, not commands.

This matters for two reasons. The controller gives the agent a sane starting point, so early training isn't spent rediscovering that legs should stay underneath the body. And because the learned component is only a correction term, the failure mode when the policy encounters something unfamiliar is degradation toward the PD controller's behavior rather than immediate collapse.

Training

Training uses PPO through Stable-Baselines3, with runs tracked in TensorBoard. Most of the iteration hasn't been on hyperparameters — it's been on the reward function.

Reward shaping is where this kind of project actually lives. An agent will find every loophole a reward leaves open: reward forward velocity alone and it learns to fall forward productively; penalize falling too hard and it learns to stand still forever. Each of those is a bug in what I asked for, not in what it did.

Where it stands

  • Custom Gymnasium environment with a URDF-modeled 6-DOF biped
  • Actuator-realistic action clamping in place from the start
  • PPO residual policy over a PD baseline, training stably
  • Straight-line walking; turning and uneven terrain are next

The eventual goal is putting a version of this on physical hardware. That's the point at which every simplifying assumption in the simulator gets audited at once, which is why the constraints went in early.
